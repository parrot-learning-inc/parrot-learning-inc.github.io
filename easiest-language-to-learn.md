# The easiest language to learn for English speakers

The easiest language to learn for English speakers is one of the FSI's Category I languages: Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, French, Romanian, Dutch, Norwegian, Swedish, or Danish. They take about 24-30 weeks of full-time class. Of those, Spanish is the best pick for most people, because it is easy and you can use it almost everywhere.
{: .lede }

## How we ranked them

The U.S. Foreign Service Institute (FSI) trains diplomats and publishes how long its students need to reach professional working proficiency in each language. The full list is on the [State Department's foreign language training page](https://www.state.gov/foreign-language-training/). The FSI groups languages into four categories, from about 24 weeks (Category I) to about 88 weeks (Category IV).

FSI students study full time in small classes, so read the hours as a comparison, not as your schedule. The FSI does not rank languages inside a category. We ordered each group by how easy the language is to start and how much you can use it.

## The 15 easiest languages to learn

| # | Language | FSI category | About class hours | Why it is easy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Spanish | I | 600 | Spelling matches the sounds, thousands of shared Latin words, more free content than you can watch |
| 2 | Portuguese | I | 600 | Grammar and vocabulary close to Spanish; nasal vowels and the Brazil vs Portugal accent gap take practice |
| 3 | Italian | I | 600 | Clear vowels, spelling matches the sounds, lots of Latin words you already know |
| 4 | Dutch | I | 600 | Germanic like English, many words look alike (*water*, *appel*, *boek*); the throaty g takes practice |
| 5 | Norwegian | I | 600 | Verbs do not change for person (*jeg er*, *du er*, *han er*), word order close to English |
| 6 | Swedish | I | 600 | Simple verbs, word order close to English; a sing-song pitch accent to learn |
| 7 | Danish | I | 600 | Grammar close to Norwegian; pronunciation is the hard part, with many swallowed sounds |
| 8 | Romanian | I | 600 | Latin vocabulary, spelling matches the sounds; it keeps some noun cases |
| 9 | French | I | 750 | Huge shared vocabulary with English; spelling and sounds do not match well, so it takes 30 weeks, not 24 |
| 10 | Afrikaans | I (older FSI lists) | 600 | No grammatical gender, verbs do not change for person, close to Dutch |
| 11 | German | II | 900 | Close cousin of English with many shared words; four cases, three genders, and verb-final clauses |
| 12 | Indonesian | II | 900 | Latin alphabet, no tones, no verb conjugation, no grammatical gender |
| 13 | Malay | II | 900 | Very close to Indonesian, with the same easy grammar |
| 14 | Swahili | II | 900 | Latin alphabet, spelling matches the sounds, regular grammar; noun classes take time |
| 15 | Haitian Creole | II | 900 | Vocabulary mostly from French, verbs do not change form |

French takes 30 weeks because of its spelling and sounds. All the other Category I languages take 24. Afrikaans appeared in Category I on older FSI lists, but it may not be on the current page, so check the source.

## What makes a language easy for you

The FSI list assumes a native English speaker who starts from zero. Your own starting point can move a language up or down the list:

- **Languages you already know.** If you speak Spanish, Portuguese and Italian come fast. If you speak German, Dutch and the Scandinavian languages feel familiar.
- **What you hear around you.** A language you hear at work, at home, or in your town gives you free practice every day.
- **Content you enjoy.** If you love the shows, music, or games in a language, you will spend more hours with it without trying.
- **A clear reason.** A trip on the calendar or a person you want to talk to keeps you going on bad days.

So an "easy" language is not only about grammar. It is the language you will actually spend time with.

## Why Spanish is the easiest useful language

Many languages are easy. Spanish is easy *and* useful in a lot of places. That combination is rare.

| Reason | What it means for you |
|---|---|
| About 500 million native speakers | It is one of the most spoken native languages in the world, so you will meet Spanish speakers everywhere |
| Official in about 20 countries, plus Puerto Rico | Spain, Mexico, most of Central and South America, Cuba, the Dominican Republic, and Equatorial Guinea |
| Spelling matches the sounds | Learn the [alphabet in Spanish](/alphabet-in-spanish/) once, and you can read any word aloud |
| Shared Latin vocabulary | You can guess thousands of words on day one (see the table below) |
| Lots of free content | Series, films, music, podcasts, news, and YouTube from many countries, in many accents |
| Close to home for U.S. learners | Millions of people in the United States speak Spanish, so you can practice locally |

Spanish is also an official language of the United Nations and the European Union.

English and Spanish share so many Latin roots that whole word endings swap in a pattern:

| English ending | Spanish ending | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| -tion | -ción | nation → nación, information → información |
| -ty | -dad | city → ciudad, university → universidad |
| -ous | -oso | famous → famoso, delicious → delicioso |
| -ly | -mente | rapidly → rápidamente, finally → finalmente |
| -ist | -ista | artist → artista, dentist → dentista |
| -ible / -able | -ible / -able | possible → posible, probable → probable |

## The easiest language by goal

The easiest language for you depends on what you want to do with it. Here is how the easy options compare by goal:

| Goal | Easiest good pick | Why | Also consider |
|---|---|---|---|
| Travel | Spanish | Works in Spain, Mexico, and most of Central and South America | Italian or Portuguese if you go to one country again and again |
| Career (in the U.S.) | Spanish | Health care, education, sales, and service jobs serve Spanish speakers every day | French for international organizations |
| Media | Spanish | A huge output of series, films, music, and podcasts from many countries | French for film, Portuguese for Brazilian music and TV |
| Family or partner | The language they speak | You get daily practice and a strong reason to keep going | Nothing else comes close |
| Living in Europe | The local language | Dutch, Swedish, and Danish are easy, but many locals switch to English, so practice takes effort | German (Category II) for Germany, Austria, and Switzerland |
| Southeast Asia | Indonesian or Malay | Latin alphabet, no tones, no verb conjugation | Tagalog (Category III) for the Philippines |
| Africa | Swahili or French | Swahili is widely used in East Africa, French in much of West and Central Africa | Portuguese for Angola and Mozambique |

🎯 **Key point:** If you do not have a family, a partner, or a move pulling you toward a specific language, start with Spanish. It is the shortest path from zero to a language you can use in many countries.

## Honest notes on the other easy languages

All of these are good choices. Here is what you should know before you pick one.

- **Norwegian:** Maybe the easiest grammar on this list. There are two written standards (Bokmål and Nynorsk) and many dialects, but most learners start with Bokmål.
- **Dutch:** Many words look like English or German. Most Dutch people speak excellent English, so you may need to ask them to stay in Dutch.
- **Afrikaans:** Very simple grammar, close to Dutch. It is spoken mostly in South Africa and Namibia, so there are fewer courses and less content.
- **Italian:** Beautiful sounds that match the spelling, and great food vocabulary. It is spoken in fewer countries than Spanish.
- **Portuguese:** If you learn Spanish first, Portuguese comes fast (and the other way around). Brazilian and European Portuguese sound quite different.
- **French:** A big shared vocabulary with English, but spelling and pronunciation do not match well. Useful across Europe, Canada, and much of Africa.
- **Romanian:** A Latin language with some Slavic words and a few noun cases. Fewer learning materials than the other Romance languages.
- **Swedish:** Simple verbs and friendly word order. The pitch accent sounds like singing at first.
- **Danish:** Grammar is easy, but the spoken language swallows many sounds. Reading Danish is much easier than understanding it.

## Should you skip the hard languages?

Not if one of them matters to you. A language you care about beats an easy one you drop after a month. But an easy language makes a great first foreign language. You learn how to learn: how to listen, how to review words, and how to keep going when you hit a wall. The second language then goes faster, whatever it is.
