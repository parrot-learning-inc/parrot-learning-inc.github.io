# The alphabet in Spanish: 27 letters and how to say them

The alphabet in Spanish has 27 letters: the 26 you already know, plus ñ. Spanish spelling is close to phonetic, so once you learn these sounds you can read almost any word out loud. Ch, ll, and rr are letter pairs (digraphs), not separate letters.

## The 27 letters of the Spanish alphabet

Here is every letter with its name, its sound, an English sound-alike, and one example word. The names follow the [Real Academia Española](https://www.rae.es/) (RAE), the body that sets Spanish spelling rules. Stressed syllables are in capitals.

You will also hear other names for a few letters: **be larga** or **be alta** for b, **ve** or **ve corta** for v, **doble ve** or **doble u** for w, and **i griega** for y. The RAE recommends the names in the table, but everyone understands the others.

🎯 **Key point:** Older textbooks list 29 or 30 letters, because they count ch, ll, and sometimes rr. Since 2010 the RAE counts 27. The sounds did not change, only the count.

## The 5 vowels

Spanish has five vowel sounds, and each one stays the same in every word. They are short and clean. They never slide into a second sound, like the English "o" in "go" (which ends in a small "oo").

| Vowel | Sound | Like English | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| a | ah | father | casa (KAH-sah), house |
| e | eh | bet | mesa (MEH-sah), table |
| i | ee | see | sí (see), yes |
| o | oh | more | solo (SOH-loh), alone |
| u | oo | food | mucho (MOO-choh), a lot |

English turns unstressed vowels into a lazy "uh". Spanish never does. Chocolate is choh-koh-LAH-teh, four clear syllables. When i or u sits next to another vowel, the two blend into one syllable: bueno (BWEH-noh), tiempo (TYEHM-poh), aire (AY-reh).

## The tricky sounds

### R vs RR

This pair changes meaning: **pero** (but) vs **perro** (dog), **caro** (expensive) vs **carro** (car).

- **Single r** is a quick tap of the tongue, like the "tt" in American "butter" said fast.
- **Rolled r (trill)** is a few fast taps in a row. You use it for rr, for r at the start of a word (rosa, rápido), and for r after n, l, or s (Enrique, alrededor, Israel).

The trill takes most adults a few weeks of practice. Until then, a strong tap is fine. People will understand you.

### J and G

J always sounds like a throaty h: **jamón** (hah-MOHN), ham. G sounds the same before e or i: **gente** (HEN-teh), people. Before a, o, u, or a consonant, g is hard like in "go": **gato**, **gracias**. In Spain this throaty sound is rough, like "ch" in Scottish "loch". In much of Latin America and the Caribbean it is softer, close to an English h.

### LL and Y

Most speakers say ll and y the same way, like the y in "yes": **llamar** (yah-MAHR), to call; **yo** (yoh), I. This is called *yeísmo*, and it is normal in most of Spain and Latin America. Regional variants:

- **Argentina and Uruguay:** ll and y sound like "sh" or the "s" in "measure". *Yo* sounds like "sho", and *calle* (street) sounds like "KAH-sheh".
- **Some areas** (parts of the Andes, Paraguay, and some rural parts of Spain) keep ll as a separate "ly" sound, close to the "lli" in "million".
- **Many speakers** say y closer to the j in "jam" at the start of a word or when they stress it: *¡Yo!*

### C and Z: Spain vs Latin America

In Latin America, the Canary Islands, and parts of southern Spain, z and soft c (before e or i) sound like s. So *casa* (house) and *caza* (hunting) sound the same. In most of Spain, they sound like the "th" in "think": *cena* is THEH-nah and *zapato* is thah-PAH-toh. The letter names change too: *ce* is "theh" and *zeta* is "THEH-tah". This is not a lisp, because the s stays an s. Both accents are correct. Pick one and stay with it.

### Silent H

H makes no sound: **hola** (OH-lah), **hablar** (ah-BLAHR), **ahora** (ah-OH-rah), now. You only hear an h-like sound in ch and in a few foreign words like *hámster*.

### B and V

B and v sound the same in Spanish. At the start of a phrase or after m or n, they sound like an English b: **vino** (BEE-noh), wine. Between vowels, the sound is softer, and your lips almost touch. Never use the English v, with your top teeth on your lip. To tell them apart when spelling, people say **be de burro** (b as in donkey) or **uve de vaca** (v as in cow).

### Ñ

Ñ is its own letter, and it comes after n in the dictionary. It sounds like the "ny" in "canyon": **mañana** (mah-NYAH-nah), tomorrow; **España** (ehs-PAH-nyah), Spain. Do not drop the little wave. *Año* means year. *Ano* means anus. "Feliz año nuevo" is a nice wish. The other version is not.

## The digraphs: ch, ll, rr, qu, gu

A digraph is two letters that make one sound. None of these count as letters, but you need all of them to read.

| Digraph | Sound | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ch | ch in "church" | chico (CHEE-koh), boy |
| ll | y in "yes" ("sh" in Argentina and Uruguay) | lluvia (YOO-byah), rain |
| rr | rolled r (trill) | perro (PEH-rroh), dog |
| qu | k, the u is silent (only before e or i) | queso (KEH-soh), cheese; quiero (KYEH-roh), I want |
| gu | hard g, the u is silent (only before e or i) | guitarra (gee-TAH-rrah), guitar; guerra (GEH-rrah), war |

When the u in *gue* or *gui* must be pronounced, it gets two dots (ü): **pingüino** (peen-GWEE-noh), penguin; **vergüenza** (behr-GWEHN-sah), shame. Native Spanish words do not use k. They write the k sound as c (ca, co, cu) or qu (que, qui).

## Stress and accent marks in short

Spanish tells you where the stress goes. Learn three rules:

| Rule | Stress falls on | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Word ends in a vowel, n, or s | the second-to-last syllable | CA-sa, HA-blan, za-PA-tos |
| Word ends in any other consonant | the last syllable | ha-BLAR, ciu-DAD, pa-PEL |
| Word has a written accent (á, é, í, ó, ú) | the accented vowel, always | ca-FÉ, can-CIÓN, ÁR-bol, MÚ-si-ca |

A written accent shows up when a word breaks the first two rules. Accents also do three other jobs:

- **They tell short words apart:** tú (you) and tu (your), él (he) and el (the), sí (yes) and si (if), mí (me) and mi (my).
- **They mark question words:** qué, cómo, dónde, cuándo, quién.
- **They split a vowel pair into two syllables:** día (DEE-ah), país (pah-EES).

Capital letters keep their accents too: *Álvaro*, *ÉXITO*.

## How to spell your name aloud in Spanish

At a hotel desk or on the phone, someone will ask you how to spell your name. These are the phrases you need:

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| ¿Cómo se escribe? | How do you spell it? |
| Se escribe... | It is spelled... |
| ¿Me lo puede deletrear? | Can you spell it for me? (formal) |
| con mayúscula | with a capital letter |
| doble ese / dos eses | double s |
| con acento / con tilde | with an accent mark |
| guion, espacio | hyphen, space |
| arroba, punto | @, dot (for email addresses) |

Some examples:

| Name | Spelled aloud |
|---|---|
| Sarah | ese, a, erre, a, hache |
| Michael | eme, i, ce, hache, a, e, ele |
| Jessica | jota, e, doble ese, i, ce, a |
| Liam | ele, i, a, eme |

💡 **Tip:** For letters that sound alike on the phone, add a word: "be de Barcelona", "uve de Valencia", "ce de casa", "ese de sol", "zeta de zapato". People in Spain and Latin America do this all the time.

## A 5-minute daily practice

You do not need a long session. Do this once a day for two weeks, and the sounds start to feel normal.

1. **Minute 1: vowels.** Say a, e, i, o, u five times, short and clean. Then say *mamá, mesa, mitad, moto, mucho*.
2. **Minute 2: one tricky pair.** Pick one pair per day: pero and perro, caro and carro, or hola and ola (they sound the same, because h is silent).
3. **Minute 3: stress.** Read ten words aloud and put the stress where the rules say. Check the audio on [SpanishDict](https://www.spanishdict.com/).
4. **Minute 4: listen and copy.** Play one short clip from a native speaker and repeat each sentence right after it.
