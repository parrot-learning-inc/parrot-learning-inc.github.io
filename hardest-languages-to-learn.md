# The hardest languages to learn for English speakers

The hardest languages to learn for native English speakers are Arabic, Mandarin, Cantonese, Japanese, and Korean. The U.S. Foreign Service Institute (FSI) puts them in its hardest group, at about 88 weeks of full-time class. Spanish sits at the other end, at about 24 weeks.
{: .lede }

## How the FSI measures "hard"

The FSI trains U.S. diplomats in foreign languages. It publishes how long its students usually need to reach professional working proficiency, which is good enough to do a job in the language. You can see the list on the [State Department's foreign language training page](https://www.state.gov/foreign-language-training/).

Keep the context in mind. FSI students study full time in small classes, do self-study on top, and many already speak another language. So read the numbers as a comparison between languages, not as your personal schedule.

| FSI category | Full-time weeks | About class hours | Languages |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | 24-30 | 600-750 | Danish, Dutch, French, Italian, Norwegian, Portuguese, Romanian, Spanish, Swedish |
| II | 36 | 900 | German, Haitian Creole, Indonesian, Malay, Swahili |
| III | 44 | 1,100 | Russian, Polish, Greek, Hindi, Turkish, Vietnamese, Thai, and many more |
| IV | 88 | 2,200 | Arabic, Chinese (Cantonese), Chinese (Mandarin), Japanese, Korean |

The FSI gives time in weeks. Hour totals you see online vary a little, because sources count a different number of class hours per week. Treat all hours on this page as "about".

⚠️ **Watch out:** The FSI does not rank languages inside a category. Every Category III language gets the same 44 weeks. Any list that puts Hungarian at number 6 and Polish at number 18 is an opinion, and that includes ours.
{: .warn }

## The 20 hardest languages to learn

The first five are the FSI's Category IV. The other fifteen are Category III languages that stack several hard features at once, like a new script plus tones, or a new script plus many noun cases.

| # | Language | FSI category | About class hours | Writing system | What makes it hard |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Arabic | IV | 2,200 | Arabic script, right to left | Short vowels usually not written, sounds made deep in the throat, big gaps between Modern Standard Arabic and the spoken dialects |
| 2 | Japanese | IV | 2,200 | Hiragana, katakana, and kanji | Three scripts at once, about 2,000 common kanji, verb at the end, politeness levels built into the grammar |
| 3 | Mandarin Chinese | IV | 2,200 | Chinese characters | Four tones plus a neutral tone, thousands of characters, almost no shared words with English |
| 4 | Cantonese | IV | 2,200 | Traditional Chinese characters | Six tones (some count nine), written Chinese differs from how people speak, fewer courses than Mandarin |
| 5 | Korean | IV | 2,200 | Hangul | Hangul is easy, but verb-final word order, speech levels, and long chains of verb endings take years |
| 6 | Hungarian | III | 1,100 | Latin | Around 18 cases, vowel harmony, very few shared roots with English |
| 7 | Finnish | III | 1,100 | Latin | 15 cases, consonants that change inside words, long compound words |
| 8 | Estonian | III | 1,100 | Latin | 14 cases, three lengths for vowels and consonants |
| 9 | Georgian | III | 1,100 | Georgian alphabet | Long consonant clusters, verbs that mark subject and object at the same time |
| 10 | Mongolian | III | 1,100 | Cyrillic in Mongolia, traditional script in China | Vowel harmony, cases, sounds that are new to English speakers |
| 11 | Thai | III | 1,100 | Thai script | Five tones, 44 consonant letters, no spaces between words |
| 12 | Vietnamese | III | 1,100 | Latin with many accent marks | Six tones in the northern standard, many vowel sounds |
| 13 | Burmese | III | 1,100 | Burmese script | Tones, a round script with stacked letters, verb-final word order |
| 14 | Amharic | III | 1,100 | Ge'ez (Ethiopic) script | More than 200 symbols to learn, new consonant sounds, words built from roots |
| 15 | Hindi | III | 1,100 | Devanagari | New consonant contrasts, verb-final word order, verbs that change for gender |
| 16 | Hebrew | III | 1,100 | Hebrew script, right to left | Vowels usually not written, words built from three-letter roots |
| 17 | Russian | III | 1,100 | Cyrillic | Six cases, verb pairs for aspect, stress that moves and is not marked |
| 18 | Polish | III | 1,100 | Latin with accent marks | Seven cases, dense consonant clusters (szcz), verb aspect |
| 19 | Icelandic | III | 1,100 | Latin with þ and ð | Four cases, three genders, few learning materials |
| 20 | Greek | III | 1,100 | Greek alphabet | New alphabet, cases, three genders (but English borrowed many Greek roots) |

## Which of the hardest five is easiest to start?

All five Category IV languages take about the same time to reach a professional level. The first few months feel very different, though:

- **Korean:** You can read Hangul after a few days of practice, and K-dramas and music give you endless listening. The grammar is where the time goes.
- **Japanese:** The sounds are easy for English speakers, with five clear vowels much like Spanish. The writing system is the long road.
- **Mandarin:** Verbs never change, and there are no plural endings or genders. Tones and characters are the hard part, from day one.
- **Arabic:** First you choose between Modern Standard Arabic (news, books) and a dialect (daily life), for example Egyptian or Levantine. Many learners end up doing both.
- **Cantonese:** It has fewer courses and apps than Mandarin, so you need more help from native speakers.

## What "hard" really means

"Hard" is not a property of a language. Every language on this page is learned by millions of toddlers. "Hard" means "far from what you already know". For an English speaker, four things add the most time:

| Factor | Why it costs time | Harder examples | Easier examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Distance from English | Fewer shared words and patterns to lean on | Hungarian, Korean, Mandarin | Dutch, Spanish, French |
| Writing system | You learn to read from zero, sometimes with thousands of symbols | Japanese, Mandarin, Arabic | Spanish, Indonesian, Swahili (Latin alphabet) |
| Sounds and tones | Pitch can change the meaning, and new sounds take ear training | Cantonese, Thai, Vietnamese | Spanish (5 vowels, no tones) |
| Grammar | Cases, verb-final order, politeness levels, noun classes | Finnish, Polish, Japanese | Indonesian, Norwegian (verbs do not change for person) |

A language can be hard in one area and easy in another. Mandarin verbs never change form. Vietnamese uses the Latin alphabet. Korean's Hangul is so logical that many learners read it after a few days of practice. The hard part is the total, not each piece.

## Why hard is not impossible

The FSI numbers are for professional level, where you could do your job in the language. That is far beyond ordering food, making friends, or watching a show. The FSI does not publish hours for basic conversation, so we will not invent a number. It is much less than the full figure, in every category.

What moves you forward in any language is the same:

- **A reason you care about.** A partner, a family, a job, or a country you love will carry you through Category IV. Boredom will stop you in Category I.
- **Lots of input you understand.** Listening and reading at your level, every day, builds the language faster than memorizing rules.
- **Daily contact.** Twenty minutes a day beats three hours on Sunday.
- **Speaking early.** Mistakes are part of it. Nobody remembers yours.

If you want to learn Japanese, learn Japanese. A hard language you love beats an easy one you quit.

## Where Spanish sits: one of the easiest

Spanish is in Category I, at about 24 weeks or about 600 class hours. Japanese, Korean, Arabic, and Mandarin need about 2,200. That is nearly four times as long for the same level.

Why Spanish is so much faster for English speakers:

- **The spelling matches the sounds.** Learn the [alphabet in Spanish](/alphabet-in-spanish/) once and you can read any word aloud.
- **You already know thousands of words.** *Hospital, animal, chocolate, información, posible, natural.* English took a large part of its vocabulary from Latin and French, and Spanish comes from Latin too.
- **The grammar has rules that mostly hold.** Verbs change a lot, but in regular patterns.
- **There is a huge amount of content.** Series, music, podcasts, and YouTube from Spain and all of Latin America.
